# Before and after

Five pairs in the site's field. Each "before" is the kind of paragraph a machine writes by default.
Each "after" keeps the same content and says it in the house voice. The notes say what changed.

Where an "after" uses a first-hand case, it is a placeholder showing the shape. In a real piece the
case must be one the author actually has; mark it `[OWNER: ...]` if it is missing.

---

## 1. PRDs

**Before**

> In today's fast-paced product landscape, a well-crafted Product Requirements Document (PRD) is
> crucial for aligning stakeholders and ensuring seamless execution. It's important to note that a
> robust PRD not only defines what to build, but also empowers teams to make informed decisions.
> Whether you're a seasoned PM or just starting out, mastering the art of the PRD can be a
> game-changer for your product's success.

**After**

> A PRD (product requirements document: the page that says what you are building and why) has one
> job. It lets an engineer make the small decisions without you in the room. Most PRDs fail that
> job because they are written for the approval meeting. They get signed, then nobody opens them
> again. I have written both kinds. The ones that worked were one page long and listed the
> decisions already made.

**What changed:** the warm-up and the audience framing are gone. The term gets its plain meaning.
The job is stated in one sentence, the failure is a short story, and the author speaks once, with
a specific (one page, decisions listed).

## 2. Usability testing

**Before**

> Usability testing is a powerful method that can help you unlock valuable insights into how users
> interact with your product. By observing real users, teams can identify pain points, uncover
> hidden issues, and ultimately create more intuitive, user-friendly, and delightful experiences.
> Let's dive into the key steps to run an effective usability test.

**After**

> A usability test is simple. You give five people a task, watch them try it, and keep quiet.
> Five is enough to find the problems that stop most people; the sixth person mostly shows you the
> same ones again. The hard part is the keeping quiet. On one checkout redesign I watched a
> designer explain the button to the third tester. We learned nothing from that session.

**What changed:** "powerful", "unlock", "valuable insights", the triad of adjectives and "Let's
dive in" are gone. The method is said in three actions. The number five comes with its reason.
The hard part is named, with a short real scene.

## 3. OKRs

**Before**

> OKRs (Objectives and Key Results) are a goal-setting framework used by leading companies like
> Google and Intel. When implemented correctly, OKRs can drive alignment, foster accountability,
> and enable teams to focus on what truly matters. However, it's worth noting that OKRs aren't a
> one-size-fits-all solution — they require careful planning, buy-in, and ongoing commitment.

**After**

> OKRs (objectives and key results) are a goal and two to four numbers that tell you whether you
> reached it. They help when several teams need to pull the same way. They hurt when one small
> team already knows its goal, because the quarterly ritual costs more focus than it buys. I would
> not run them in a team of fewer than about eight people. Write the goal on a sticky note instead.

**What changed:** the name-dropping, the triad of verbs, "truly", "it's worth noting" and the dash
are gone. The fake balance ("not one-size-fits-all") becomes a call with a reason and a number.
The piece gives the reader something to do.

## 4. Retention

**Before**

> Customer retention is a vital metric that every product team should be tracking. In the
> ever-evolving world of SaaS, retaining customers is often more cost-effective than acquiring new
> ones. By leveraging data-driven insights and implementing targeted engagement strategies, teams
> can significantly boost retention rates and drive sustainable growth.

**After**

> Retention is the share of people who come back. Measure it by week for the first eight weeks
> after sign-up, one line per sign-up week. If the lines flatten, some people found a reason to
> stay; find out who they are. If the lines slide to zero, no amount of marketing will fix it.
> Look at the chart below: the March line flattens at 22%. That is the group to interview.

**What changed:** "vital", "ever-evolving", "leveraging data-driven insights" and "drive
sustainable growth" carried no content and are gone. The term is defined in seven words. The
method is concrete (by week, eight weeks, one line per cohort), the reader gets two signals and
what each means, and the picture is shown, then interpreted.

## 5. Hiring a product designer

**Before**

> Hiring the right product designer is one of the most important decisions you'll make. A great
> designer brings creativity, empathy, and strategic thinking to the table. In this comprehensive
> guide, we'll explore the essential steps to find, evaluate, and onboard top design talent. Here
> are 10 tips to help you navigate the hiring process with confidence!

**After**

> The portfolio tells you how a designer's best project looked. It does not tell you how they
> decide. So I stopped asking candidates to present their portfolio and started asking them to
> walk through one decision they got wrong. In the last two rounds I ran, that one question
> separated the shortlist faster than the take-home task did. This guide is the interview plan I
> use now, with the questions, what a good answer sounds like and what to skip.

**What changed:** the "most important decision" claim, the triad, "comprehensive guide",
"essential", "navigate", "10 tips" and the exclamation mark are gone. The piece opens on an
insight, says what the author changed and why, and promises a usable thing (the interview plan).
