# How the reference pieces are written

Read on 9 Oct 2026, before writing the content-writing skill. Sources: the two course lessons the owner
shared (screenshots in this folder) and five explainers from the owner's other projects (Printables:
how-it-works, origin-lock-explained, wordpress-stack; Ai-trading: collector-walkthrough, setup-checklist).

## The shape of a piece

1. A promise up front, in two or three short lines: what you get and how long it takes. "A working folder
   that changes how the assistant responds to you. Three files. Five minutes."
2. The why, told as a short story of the problem: what people do today, where it goes wrong, one line that
   names the fix. "The folder fixes that."
3. Permission to skip: what this piece does not cover and that you do not need it yet. "You do not need
   any of that to start."
4. The how, as numbered steps, one action each, with the thing itself shown (a file, a command, a table)
   and one line under it saying what to change. "Replace the brackets with your info. Keep it short."
5. A stop line when the work is done. "That is it. Three files."
6. A reflection: what just happened and why it worked, in plain words.
7. A way on: go deeper, next up, related pages. Links name the piece and say what it holds.

The explainers use the same spine with pictures: numbered sections, one idea and one picture each, an
analogy that carries the whole page (a library, a concert venue, a camera and a vault, a layer cake),
then an "In real life" list that maps every part of the analogy to the real thing, and a one-line
"Rule" box as the takeaway. "Look at the drawings first; the words are just labels."

## The sentences

- Short. Many under ten words. Fragments are allowed when they land a point. "Three files. Five minutes."
- One idea per sentence, one point per paragraph, paragraphs of one to three sentences.
- Present tense, second person. "You give the assistant a small set of files."
- Plain words. Every technical term gets its plain meaning next to it, the first time, in the same
  sentence or the next. "Use .md (markdown) if you can. Plain .txt works too."
- Concrete over abstract: a number, a file name, a button label, a time. "About 10 minutes." "Click the
  blue Add record button above the table."
- Declaratives, not hedges. "The folder is memory. The prompt is direction. They work together."
- No exclamation marks. Almost no rhetorical questions. No adjectives doing the work of evidence.

## The voice

- A person who has done this many times, talking to one reader, calmly. Confident without selling.
- Explains the why before the how, and says what to leave out. That is where experience shows.
- Reassures about scope. "This is the quick version. We are getting you a win right now."
- Admits the messy parts plainly. "Less elegant. Works fine."
- Shows, then interprets. Never describes what a reader can look at.
- Tells the reader what to check. "Check it worked: open a terminal and run ..."

## What marks it as not machine-written

- No warm-up paragraph that defines the topic before saying anything.
- No "In today's fast-paced world", "Let's dive in", "In conclusion", "It's important to note",
  "Whether you're a ... or a ...", "game-changer", "seamless", "robust", "leverage", "unlock".
- No lists of seven generic tips. Lists appear only when the items are parallel actions or parts.
- No bold on every other phrase. Bold marks a label or a file name, not emphasis.
- No closing summary that restates the piece. The piece ends on the next step.
- No triads of adjectives, no mirrored sentence pairs, no chains of dashes.
- Specific nouns and numbers everywhere a generic word could sit.

## What our site adds to it

- The author's fifteen years: first person is allowed and wanted. "I have watched this fail three ways."
  Opinions stated as opinions, with the reason.
- US English and US spelling.
- Every piece ends with something usable: a template, a checklist, a question list, a download.
- Headings in the words people search; the first paragraph answers the title's question.
