The publishing system, as a newsroom: proposal 2

A proposal for how articles get made. A small newsroom of agents finds ideas, then writes, draws and checks each piece and gets it ready. Once the site is live, a campaign manager watches how it performs. You walk in on Sunday as the publisher, make the calls and press the button.

October 9, 2026. A proposal for discussion: nothing on this page is built yet.

This replaces proposal 1. What changed: a fifth desk, Performance, where a campaign manager watches, scores, nudges and schedules refreshes, and never edits the site.

Think of the system as a magazine newsroom with a weekly deadline.

The one-screen version

The system is a small newsroom of agents. An agent is a program that does one job and follows a written rulebook.

  • An explorer keeps a backlog (a list of article ideas waiting to be written) full.
  • On Sunday you open the backlog, approve ideas, say “write six”, and give a story where one is asked for.
  • The desks write, draw, check and prepare each article. They stop twice for your eyes.
  • A publisher puts the article in WordPress and checks the live page.
  • Adapters turn it into a LinkedIn post, and later a short video reel.
  • A campaign manager reads the numbers every week, scores the site, each category and each article against what we expected, nudges with evidence and keeps old pages current. It proposes and never changes the site.
10 agentsin the first build. 11 with reels, 12 with the campaign manager after launch.
1 to 2 hoursof your time on Sunday, plus a short review mid-week.

1. The newsroom at a glance

Five desks sit around one board. The board holds the backlog of ideas. Four desks pass the work along, and the fifth, Performance, watches the live site and sends nudges back. One person in the middle hands every job from desk to desk.

In real life

  • Planning desk: the explorer, which finds and ranks ideas.
  • Making desk: seven agents. Researcher, writer, illustrator, thumbnail maker, checker, SEO reviewer and editor.
  • Publishing desk: the publisher, which puts the article into WordPress and checks the live page.
  • Distribution desk: the LinkedIn writer now, the reel maker later.
  • Performance desk: the campaign manager, after launch. It reads the site’s numbers every week, writes nudges for the board and the editor, and decides when an old page needs a refresh.
  • Managing editor: the main session, the conversation you have with the assistant. It hands work between agents and runs the progress page.

One rule shapes the room. Agents cannot call each other directly. When the writer needs a figure, it asks the managing editor, and the managing editor sends the job to the illustrator.

That is one level flatter than desks talking to desks. Every hand-off shows up in one place, the progress page, where you can see it. The campaign manager’s nudges follow the same rule: they are written down on the board, never sent straight to a desk.

Rule Every hand-off passes the managing editor, so you can always see who is doing what.

2. The backlog is the heart of the room

Every article starts as one line on the board. A dry board leaves the desks idle. A board full of weak ideas gives you weak articles. So the backlog gets two water marks and a gate.

In real life

  • The board is a file you can open: research/editorial/backlog.csv, with a readable view, backlog.html.
  • Top water mark: keep at least 20 approved ideas.
  • Bottom water mark: when approved ideas drop below 12, the explorer runs again.
  • The gate: an idea without a believable learning sentence is dropped, whatever its search volume.

The learning sentence is the test. It reads: “the reader learns X, which the top five pages do not teach.” If the explorer cannot write that sentence honestly, the idea does not get on the board.

Volume (searches a month) and difficulty (how hard it is to reach Google’s first page) put the ideas that pass in order. They never let an idea in.

What one line on the board holds

An example line, made up for this page
Working title
How to write a PRD engineers read to the end
Reader’s question
How long should a PRD be?
Learning sentence
The reader learns to cut a product requirements document (PRD) to one page in five sections, which the top five pages do not teach: they hand out 12-section templates.
Needs your story
Yes: a PRD of yours that nobody read past page two.
Expectation
Top 10 for “how long should a PRD be” within 90 days of publishing, and 50 clicks a month by day 120.
Status
idea

Rule No learning sentence, no place on the board. Search volume only decides the order.

3. How the explorer finds ideas

The explorer is the reporter on the planning desk who reads the rival papers every week. It lays three things over each other and looks for the spots where they do not match.

It reads six sources, cheapest first. The first five cost nothing.

  1. Our keyword store (research/seo/). More than 200 scored phrases, each with its intent (what the searcher wants), trend, difficulty and category. We paid for this research already. Reading it is free.
  2. Competitor sitemaps. A sitemap is a public file a site publishes that lists all its pages. We already pulled them for the competitors on our lists, in research/seo/competitors/sitemaps. Once a week the explorer pulls them again and compares with last week’s copy. Every new address becomes a line in “what they are writing now”, with its title read from the page. Where a competitor has an RSS feed (a site’s running list of new posts), the explorer reads that too. No paid service and no API (a paid data connection) are needed.
    A new competitor article tells us the topic is live. We write it only if our learning sentence beats theirs.
  3. Our cached search results. The “people also ask” questions and related searches we already stored from Google. They are reader questions in the reader’s own words.
  4. The live pages. For each candidate idea, the explorer opens the top five pages and reads them in full. It writes down what each answers, its headings, and what is missing: no example, no numbers, no story, no template, or the wrong reader. The angle and the learning sentence come from that list.
  5. The coverage map. Our own published list, counted per category. It shows which of the 13 categories are thin, which ones the plan said to launch first, and when to open an empty one. The explorer must give its reason in a sentence like this:

    “Expanding into Product marketing because Product management now has 8 pieces and Product marketing has 0.”

  6. Your story bank (research/editorial/stories.md), a file of the stories you have told. An idea that matches a story jumps the queue. Your stories are what make our piece different from the top five.

Three kinds of idea come out

A gapPeople search it. Nobody answers it well.
A betterCompetitors answer it badly, and you have a story.
An expansionA planned category with nothing in it yet.

The explorer looks for new ideas. Pages we already published belong to the campaign manager (section 8), which adds its follow-ups and refreshes to the same board.

Paid keyword checks, for phrases the store has never seen, run at most once a month, in one batch, under the spending cap you already set.

Rule A rival’s new article tells us a topic is live. We write it only when our learning sentence beats theirs.

4. Your Sunday

You are the publisher who walks in on Sunday. About half an hour of calls at the start. Then the desks work while you do something else, and you come back to read the drafts.

  1. Open the backlog page. Approve or kill ideas, and give each approved one its one-line expectation. Once the site is live, the campaign manager’s nudges and refresh jobs wait here too. About 10 minutes.
  2. Say “write six”, or any number.
  3. Give the stories. For ideas marked “needs a story”, tell it in a few lines, or paste a voice note transcript.
  4. The desks run. The progress page shows each step and chimes when a desk needs you.
  5. Read the editor’s one-page summary and the draft for each article. Say fix or approve.
  6. Press publish or schedule.

Rule The system never publishes on its own. The button is yours.

5. One article, station by station

Each approved idea rides a belt past seven stations, the way copy moves from desk to desk in a newsroom. Two stop signs hold it for your eyes. A refresh job from the campaign manager rides the same belt.

In real life

  • Researcher writes the brief: what the top pages answer, what we will answer, the structure, the sources.
  • Writer drafts in the house voice from the brief and your story. The draft ends with a list of figures needed and a thumbnail request.
  • Illustrator draws the figures with our illustration generator, in the article’s tint (its one assigned color).
  • Thumbnail maker draws the hero image and the small thumbnail with our thumbnail rulebook.
  • Checker verifies every claim, number and link against the sources, and the text against the banned-words list.
  • SEO reviewer checks the title, the slug (the short web address, like prd-template), the headings, the first paragraph, internal links and intent against the keyword store.
  • Editor signs off against one question, and writes your one-page summary.
  • Stop sign 1 comes after the brief. It is optional; you can skip it. Stop sign 2 comes after the editor, every time.

Everything for one article lives in one folder:

research/articles/<slug>/
  brief.md the researcher’s plan
  draft.md the writer’s draft
  figures/ the illustrator’s pictures
  thumbnail/ hero and thumb
  checks.md the checker’s findings
  seo-review.md the SEO reviewer’s notes
  final.md the signed-off article

You can open any stage and read it. If a run stops halfway, it picks up from the last file written.

Rule The editor asks one question: does it deliver the learning the backlog promised?

6. Publishing, then checking the live page

The press room prints the issue. Then someone pulls the first copy off the press and checks it before it goes out. The publisher does both jobs.

In real life

  • The publisher creates the draft post with WP-CLI (WordPress’s command line: setting up a post by typed commands instead of clicks). It sets the category, tags, featured image, the Rank Math title and description (what Google shows in its results) and the schedule date.
  • For a refresh, it updates the existing post instead of making a new one, and sets the “updated on” date the page shows.
  • Then it fetches the live page and checks the title, the canonical URL (the one official address of the page), images with alt text (a description for people who cannot see the image), Open Graph tags (what LinkedIn shows when the link is shared), broken links, and page speed basics.

WordPress is not installed yet. Until it is, an article’s path ends at final.md and the images.

Rule An article is done when the live page passes its check.

7. Sharing it beyond the site

One article becomes several pieces for other places. In a newsroom this is syndication: the same story, cut to fit another paper.

In real life

  • LinkedIn writer: a post in your voice (a hook, three points, the link) and a carousel (a post of swipeable slides) built from the article’s figures. LinkedIn is the main channel.
  • Reel maker, later: a 20 to 30 second script, a storyboard from the figures, a voiceover, put together with code in the same look as the thumbnails.

The honest limits

  • Posting by API (letting a program post for you) to LinkedIn and Instagram needs app approval and a business account. Instagram also wants the video on a public web address.
  • So the first version makes ready-to-post packs, and you post by hand.
  • A voiceover needs a speech service. Free ones that run on this computer sound fine for reels. Better ones cost a little per minute.

Rule The first version prepares the post. You do the posting.

8. The campaign manager

After the site is live, a campaign manager reads the numbers every week and tells the room what to change. In a newsroom this is the circulation manager, who reads the sales figures every Monday and tells the editor which pages sold.

In real life: what it reads

  • Search Console (Google’s free report on how a site does in search), for every page: impressions (how often the page showed in results), clicks, position, and the queries each page gets.
  • Site analytics: time on page, and what readers click next.
  • Core Web Vitals (Google’s three measures of how fast and steady a page feels), from the free page-speed check.
  • Our keyword store, so it knows what each article was meant to rank for.
  • The competitor sitemap changes, so it sees when a rival answers the same question.

What it writes

First, a weekly scorecard at three levels: the whole site, each category, and each article. An article is scored against the expectation set when you approved the idea.

An example article line, made up for this page

“Published 9 days ago, ranking 14 for its main phrase, zero clicks, two of its three phrases not indexed yet.”

Then a short list of nudges. Each one comes with its evidence and a proposed fix:

  • Refresh a page that slipped.
  • Rewrite a title that gets many impressions and few clicks.
  • Add internal links to a page that gets none.
  • Write the follow-up a page’s queries ask for.
  • Pause a category that is not moving, and shift the Sunday six elsewhere.
  • Flag a page that got slow.

Old pages are its pages too

The campaign manager also keeps old articles current. Every published article gets a review date, six months after publishing by default.

A trigger brings the date forward:

  • A rival publishes on the same question (seen in the sitemap changes).
  • The page slips in rank.
  • A fact, number, tool or screenshot in it goes out of date.
  • Readers’ queries on the page shift.

When a review is due, it writes a refresh job. The job says what changed in the world since the page was written, what the page now gets wrong or misses, and what to keep.

The campaign manager does not do the refresh. The job rides the making desk like a new article. The researcher checks what changed, the writer updates the piece in the house voice, the checker and the SEO reviewer go over it, and the editor signs off. You approve at the usual stop sign, and the page shows an “updated on” date.

So no separate refresh agent is needed. The campaign manager decides what and when. The making desk does the work.

The limit

It proposes. It never changes the site itself. Nudges and refresh jobs land on the backlog and on your Sunday scorecard, and you say which ones run.

When it starts

Only once the site is live and connected to Search Console, so it is the last thing we build. The expectation for each article is written from day one, though: one line in the backlog when you approve the idea. When the campaign manager arrives, every page already says what it was meant to do.

The small loop: your fixes

Every correction you make goes into a feedback file for that desk, research/editorial/feedback/<desk>.md. The agent reads it before its next run.

This is where your taste builds up. Tell the writer once that an opening is too slow, and the next draft starts faster. The same mistake does not come back.

Rule The campaign manager proposes, with evidence. You decide what runs.

11. Questions for you

Seven calls before step 1. Pick an answer for each, or give a different one in our next session.

1. Is six a week realistic?

Each story takes you about 10 minutes. Six articles with stories is about an hour of telling.

2. Water marks of 20 and 12?

Keep 20 approved ideas; refill when fewer than 12 are left.

3. Which categories may the explorer open on its own?

The rest it only proposes, and waits for you to ask.

4. Should the brief be a stop sign?

A stop after the brief catches a wrong angle early. It costs you one more look per article.

5. How often to pull competitor sitemaps?
6. Should the explorer watch more than sites?

A few LinkedIn voices or newsletters would show topics before they reach a sitemap.

7. When does the weekly scorecard reach you?

Weekly scorecard on Sunday with the backlog, or a separate mid-week note?

Your choices are remembered in this browser only. Say them in our next session to apply them.
