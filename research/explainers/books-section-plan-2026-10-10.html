The Books section: the plan

Ten topic pages at launch, each one a short, honest list of the four to six books worth reading on one subject. One shelf page leads to them. No book gets a page of its own. Six more topic pages follow, two a month. Three calls are yours to make, listed at the end.

October 10, 2026. A proposal: nothing on this page is built yet.

Think of the section as a small bookshop where every book on the shelf has a staff card under it.

1. The one-screen version

  • What it is for: curation. Each book gets who it is for, who should skip it, the part that matters and where it has aged. Your own take appears only on books you have read, and it is labeled.
  • Three levels: the shelf page (an intro and one tile per topic), the topic pages (the books as sections), and the category hubs (three books and a link).
  • Topic pages, not book pages: a search for a book's title is a shopping search, and the store answers it. A search like “ux books” is open, and small sites rank there.
  • The launch ten, in order: product management, UX design, UI design, project management, product metrics, product discovery, UX research, psychology, startups and business, leading product teams.
  • The big three need an angle. Leadership, business and personal development are big because the searches are general. Each title leads with the searched phrase and carries the angle in a searched phrase where one exists (books for new managers, books for entrepreneurs), or the page is one more list behind Harvard Business Review and the bookstores.
  • Then two a month for three months, until all sixteen are live. Adding a book is a real change, so the page's date moves, and that is fine with Google.

2. What the shelf is for: honest curation

The shelf saves a reader from buying the wrong book. That is the whole job. The line that does it most is the one on who should skip a book.

Every entry answers the same questions a good bookseller answers at the counter. Who is this for? Who will find it slow or beside the point? Which chapters matter for someone who builds products? Where has it aged: a dated tool, a benchmark from 2013, a study that did not hold up?

Your own take goes only on books you have read. It sits in its own labeled box, so a reader can tell your reading apart from the general entry. On the other books, the entry says plainly where the pick comes from: “the standard reference for interview technique”, or “on most product reading lists we checked”.

Two entries, side by side (shape only; the wording comes later)

A book you have read. What it covers, who it is for, who should skip it, where it has aged. Then a box headed From my own reading: [OWNER: what you took from it, and the chapter you would send a new PM to].

A book you have not read. The same four parts, written from the book and the lists that recommend it. No first-person line, no box.

Why the split matters: Google's raters mark down claims of personal experience that a page cannot back up. A shelf that says “I read this” on forty books would be the one thing on the site that is not true. The shelf grows honestly instead: when you read a book, its entry gains your box.

The data supports a personal list. On the 12 book searches we pulled in October, practitioner lists ranked well: Ken Norton's personal site was on page one for three of them. No large design or business publication (Smashing Magazine, Nielsen Norman Group, Harvard Business Review) ranked on any.

In real life

  • The staff card: the book's entry on its topic page (covers, for whom, skip if, the part that matters, aged).
  • A card signed by the owner of the shop: the labeled “From my own reading” box, only on books you have read.
  • An unsigned card: an entry that names where the pick comes from, with no first-person line.

Rule Say who should skip the book. Speak in the first person only about books you have read.

3. Three levels: shelf, topic, hub

The section has three kinds of page. Only one of them holds the books. The other two point at it.

The shelf page opens from the Books menu at /books/. It has a short intro in your voice: who the shelf is for, how it is grouped, and that a page says so where a book has dated. Then come the tiles, grouped Design, Product and Business. Each tile has a code-drawn thumbnail, the topic name and the count of books. No books are listed on the shelf itself.

A topic page, such as “Books on UX design”, holds four to six books as sections, simply listed. Section 7 shows one.

The category hubs are the pages that already hold everything on a subject: its articles, and later its tools. Each hub shows that subject's top three books and links to the topic page.

Two things are left out on purpose. There is no page per book (section 4 says why), and there are no tag or author archives. Those archives would be thin lists that Google indexes and nobody needs.

In real life

  • The shop window: the shelf page, with its intro and tiles.
  • One shelf in the shop: one topic page, with its books listed.
  • The “also on this subject” stand by the till: the three books on each category hub.

Rule Books live on topic pages. The shelf and the hubs only point there.

4. Why topic pages and not book pages

A topic search is a reader asking what to read. A book-title search is a buyer looking for the book. Our pages can win the first. The store wins the second, and should.

The right half is real data. For “ux books” (390 searches a month in the US), three of the nine ordinary results on October 8 were small editorial sites and two were forums. The left half is our reading, not a measurement: we did not pull page one for any book title. Amazon alone sat on page one for 11 of the 12 topic searches we did pull, so on a search for the book itself, the shop has the stronger claim.

The second reason is depth. Forty book pages would each say a few hundred words about one book. That is a thin page in Google's terms, and forty of them look like a page factory. Ten topic pages each hold four to six books and the links between them. One deep page per topic is also what a reader wants: the choice between books, not a page on one.

In real life

  • A title search: “atomic design book” is the one in our data, 90 searches a month. That reader has already chosen.
  • A topic search: “ux books”, “product management books”, “okr books”. That reader is choosing.
  • A thin page: a page with little of its own to say, made mainly to catch a search.

Rule Aim at the search where the reader is still choosing.

5. The sixteen topics by demand and openness

Demand runs from 24,360 searches a month for leadership books down to 20 for product discovery. Look at the bars first, then the circles on the right.

Three topics are big because their searches are general: leadership, business and personal development. “leadership books” alone has 14,800 searches a month, and those readers are school principals, coaches and new managers in every trade. Harvard Business Review, Harvard, the Center for Creative Leadership and the bookstores hold that first page. For “management books”, one result in the top ten was a small or weak site.

Personal development has the same problem, worse. Its wide phrases (“self help books”, 60,500 a month) are consumer shelves we will not win, and none of the top ten for “productivity books” is a small editorial site.

Business is the exception among the three. “startup books” has the most open first page of the big topics: founder blogs, Hacker News and Reddit. Its reported 8,100 a month carries a spike, though. The last few months ran 590 to 880, and the ranking uses 590.

So the big pages get an angle, and where a searched phrase carries it, the title uses that phrase. “books for new managers” (260 a month) is the winnable corner of leadership, so the title reads “Best leadership books for new managers”. “books for entrepreneurs” (1,900) does the same job for business.

In real life

  • Searches a month: Google Ads volume for the US, 90 phrases, pulled October 10. Near-identical phrases count once.
  • Open: five or more small editorial, forum or video results in the top ten. Medium: three or four. Hard: two or fewer.
  • Every one of the 18 first pages shows an AI overview, so it does not tell topics apart. Your reasons for each pick are the part a summary cannot copy.

Rule A big general search needs a title that names our reader.

6. The launch ten, with their titles

The order weighs four things: demand, how open page one is, fit with the site, and how many good books we hold. The first three are core and open. Pages 4 to 7 are core, with medium demand or strong books. Pages 8 to 10 carry the biggest general demand and lean on the angle.

Titles and descriptions are built on what people search (your rule, October 10). The title tag leads with the strongest searched phrase for the topic, such as “best product management books” or “books for entrepreneurs”. A second searched phrase or the page’s honest angle follows only if it fits under 60 characters. Audience tails nobody searches (“for product teams”, “for designers”) are gone. The meta description, 140 to 155 characters, carries the main phrase and one more searched phrase, and says who the page is for and what it promises. The page heading is the plain name. The site name goes once at the end of the title tag.

Two notes on the titles. Google usually shows about 55 to 60 characters, and the site name adds about 17 more, so the searched phrase comes first and a cut tail costs little. All sixteen title tags are 60 characters or fewer. Where Google counts phrases as one group (“product management books” and “books for product managers” are both 590, one number), the column says “same count”: the second phrase adds wording, not new searches.

The ten differ from the pure demand ranking in two places. UX research comes in, and personal development moves to month 3: its fit is the weakest of the sixteen. Product discovery comes in, and accessibility moves to month 2: discovery has almost no searches of its own, but it is core to the site and the stock is strong, while the accessibility canon is thin (four books, none that every list names).

7. What one topic page holds

A short intro, then each book as a section with the same parts in the same order. A reader learns the shape once and scans the rest.

  • What it covers: the book's argument in our own words, three or four sentences.
  • Who it is for: the reader and the stage, such as “a PM in the first two years”.
  • Who should skip it: the reader who already knows it, or who needs something else.
  • The part that matters: the chapters that matter most to a product person.
  • Where it has dated: tools, numbers or studies that no longer hold. Left out when nothing has.
  • Your take: only on books you have read, in a labeled box.
  • Read it with: links to other books, on the same page or on another topic page. Books and articles are not cross-linked for now.
  • The buy link: marked as sponsored, with a one-line disclosure beside it.

Rule Same parts, same order, for every book on every page.

8. The drip, and why updating a page is fine

Ten pages go live on launch day. Two more follow each month for three months. Between those, existing pages gain books as you read them and as the re-cut fills the thin ones.

Google is fine with pages that change. Its own advice is to keep a page current and update it as needed. What it warns against is a fake date: “changing the date of pages to make them seem fresh when the content has not substantially changed.”

A new book on a topic page is a substantial change: a new section, a new pick on the page. So the page's “Updated” line moves to that month, and so does the date Google reads (the dateModified field). A rewritten “where it has dated” note moves it too. A fixed typo or a swapped link does not.

In real life

  • The date on the page: a quiet “Updated <month year>” line, equal to dateModified.
  • Who moves it: the publisher, and only for a change a reader would notice.
  • A new topic page: it gets its own first date on the day it goes live. Nothing is backdated.

Rule A real change moves the date. Nothing else does.

9. The rules it follows

Two rulebooks apply: the search-policy rulebook and the house writing style. Here is what each one means for the shelf.

RuleWhat it means on the shelf
Sponsored links, disclosedEvery buy link is marked rel="sponsored", with a visible one-line disclosure beside it, not only in the footer.
Honest about weak spotsA recommendation that pays the site says where the book is weak. The “who should skip it” and “where it has dated” parts do that job.
No thin pagesNo page per book, no tag or author archives, and filters never make a new address. Raters mark affiliate lists built from store content with product names swapped as Lowest, even with a disclosure.
No reproduced textEvery entry is in our words. No long quotes, no invented quotes, no page numbers we have not checked. Cover images only through a route the store's license allows.
Real datesThe date moves only for a real change (section 8). Nothing is backdated.
First person only when trueYour take appears only on books you have read, in its labeled box.
House writing styleThe teacher's voice, short sentences, a plain word beside every term, nothing from the banned list. Every intro and entry goes through the checker.

Rule If a line would mislead a reader about the book or about us, it does not ship.

10. What we build, and what you decide now

The build is small: two post types, one template, and the tiles. Your three calls come first, because the titles and the re-cut depend on them.

What we build

  • A book post type in the plugin. One entry per book: title, author, year, topic, the eight parts and the buy link. It has no public page of its own; topic pages and hubs display it. Structure lives in the plugin, never in the theme.
  • A reading-path post type in the plugin. Three to five books for one situation, from the earlier plan. The type is there from the start; the paths themselves can wait until the topic pages are live.
  • The shelf template in the child theme: the intro, then the tiles grouped Design, Product and Business.
  • A topic page layout that lists its books in your order, each with the same parts.
  • Three books on each category hub, the top three of that topic page, with the link.
  • The tiles, drawn by the thumbnail generator in the house styles, one per topic page.

The shelf, the topic pages and the hubs are indexed. Book entries, tags, filters and archives are not.

Decisions for you

  1. The order of the ten. As in section 6: product management, UX design, UI design, project management, product metrics, product discovery, UX research, psychology, startups and business, leading product teams. Approve, or move pages.
  2. The swap. UX research in, personal development out to month 3 (and the same kind of swap for product discovery in, accessibility to month 2). My view: yes. Personal development has demand, but it is the weakest fit and none of its first ten results is a small editorial site.
  3. The titles and metas in section 6. Rebuilt on searched phrases, with the phrases and their volumes beside each. Approve, or change any of the sixteen.
