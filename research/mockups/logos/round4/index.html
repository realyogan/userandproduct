Logo concepts, round four

Ten concepts, each an abstract mark plus the name, judged by one test: would it look at home on the masthead of a serious journal, the door of a bank or the cover of a university press book? Weight, restraint and settled type over cleverness. Every mark is plain geometry, never an object; every name is set in an open-license typeface and outlined.

Each concept is shown at 560px on white and near-black, then where it will live: a 1280px site header strip on white and on near-black. Below that the mark, favicon renders at 32px and 16px, one color, the 400px avatar and a mock profile tile. Pin any lockup to compare side by side.

Masthead: P1 vermilion block, P2 salmon band.

Monogram in a block: P3 serif "up", P4 grotesque "u&p".

Abstract symbol: P5 square and disc, P6 weighted ring, P7 shifted halves.

Negative space: P8 sliced disc, P9 quarter cut. Own: P10 ensign.

RationaleThe newspaper move in its plainest form: a solid red block that does nothing but carry the color, and the name beside it as a nameplate, Fraunces SemiBold capitals tracked wide in ink. One detail ties the block to the type: a white rule cut through the block at the exact height of the nameplate's baseline, running out of the block's right edge toward the letters, as if the line of type started inside the block. The block alone, rule and all, is the favicon and the avatar.

Device, type, paletteRed square with one white baseline rule. Fraunces SemiBold capitals, tracked. Press Red, Ink and White.

OriginalityClosest: The Economist (white serif name inside a red box) and Purina (a small red square beside a serif name on a black panel). Ours keeps the name out of the box and gives the box one cut rule instead of text or a pattern, so the block reads as a device of its own.

DirectionMasthead: a solid red block beside a tracked serif nameplate.

Masthead or pitch deck?Masthead. A red block and a tracked serif nameplate is how newspapers have looked for a century.

RationaleThe Financial Times answer: the nameplate is printed on a band of salmon newsprint, black Fraunces Regular capitals with generous tracking, and a heavy black rule inset along the top of the band, the rule a newspaper runs under its folio line. The whole band is the logo; cut a square from its left end and you have the favicon (salmon with the black rule across the top). The band holds its shape on white and on near-black without a dark variant, which is how print brands behave.

Device, type, paletteSalmon band with an inset black rule. Fraunces Regular capitals, tracked. Salmon Paper and Black.

OriginalityClosest: the Financial Times (salmon is its color; its nameplate is black serif capitals on salmon paper, with no band or rule as a device). Ours makes the salmon a contained band with an inset rule, and sets our own name. The color is shared with a whole genre of financial papers, so the band and rule must always travel together.

DirectionMasthead: the nameplate printed on a band of salmon newsprint under a black rule.

Masthead or pitch deck?Masthead. It is a newspaper nameplate, nothing else; no startup ships a salmon band.

RationaleThe Harvard Business Review move with a serif: a heavy lowercase "up" in Fraunces SemiBold, reversed out of a deep navy square, used alone as the mark. Beside it the full name is set small and very wide in the same serif's capitals, so the square does the shouting and the name the explaining. The descender of the p hangs inside the square, the only flourish allowed.

Device, type, paletteNavy square with "up" cut out. Fraunces SemiBold (monogram and name). Deep Navy and White.

OriginalityClosest: Harvard Business Review (white serif capitals HBR in a red square). Ours uses two lowercase serif letters in navy, so it borrows the format and not the look. Generic "UP" monograms in stock sets are sans and usually interlocked; this one is set as type.

DirectionMonogram: a heavy serif "up" reversed out of a deep navy square, name small and wide.

Masthead or pitch deck?Masthead. A serif monogram block is the university-press and business-journal format exactly.

RationaleThe same tradition in a grotesque: "u&p" in Geist Bold cut out of a solid black disc, with the full name in Geist SemiBold capitals at small size and very wide tracking. The ampersand is the joining word of the name, said once, inside the seal. Black and white only, so it prints anywhere and the disc is unmistakable as an avatar.

Device, type, paletteBlack disc with "u&p" cut out. Geist Bold monogram, Geist SemiBold name. Black and White.

OriginalityClosest: the black-disc monogram seals common among law firms and small publishers, and HBR's letters-in-a-block format. Ours is a plain disc with no ring or border, and the ampersand between lowercase letters is uncommon among them.

DirectionMonogram: "u&p" in a heavy grotesque cut out of a black disc, name small and wide.

Masthead or pitch deck?Masthead, by a margin. The wide-tracked small capitals keep it institutional; with a tight lowercase name beside it, it would drift toward an app icon.

RationaleTwo primitives and nothing else: a black square and a blue disc of exactly the same height, side by side with a narrow gap. One is made (the square, the product), one is natural (the disc, the person); they stand level, neither on top. It means that and depicts nothing. The name in Geist Bold, tight.

Device, type, paletteSquare plus disc. Geist Bold. Ink, Signal Blue and White.

OriginalityClosest: USA Today (a solid blue disc beside the name) and the Mastercard idea of two shapes as a symbol. Ours pairs the disc with a black square and never overlaps them, which neither does.

DirectionSymbol: a black square and a blue disc of one height, side by side, not touching.

Masthead or pitch deck?Borderline masthead. Square and disc read as settled Swiss geometry, but at 16px the pair can look like media controls; it needs the wordmark at header size.

RationaleOne shape that holds: a deep green disc with its round opening set high, so the ring is thin at the crown and heavy at the base, the weight settled at the bottom like a bowl or a foundation. It reads as a ring, then as something steady. The name in Instrument Sans Bold in the same green, on cream.

Device, type, paletteDisc with an off-center opening. Instrument Sans Bold. Deep Green on Cream.

OriginalityClosest: Carraro (a black ring with angular cuts) and the One Ring style concentric annulus. Neither moves the opening off center; the off-center opening is the whole mark here.

DirectionSymbol: a ring whose opening sits high, so the weight settles at the bottom.

Masthead or pitch deck?Masthead. A single heavy ring in one deep color on cream has the calm of a university press colophon.

RationaleThe Mastercard move, restrained: one disc cut down its middle, the navy half lifted and the oxblood half lowered by the same small amount, a hair of space between. Two halves of one whole, slightly out of step: user and business, never quite aligned, always one circle. Inter Display SemiBold in navy.

Device, type, paletteTwo half discs, offset. Inter Display SemiBold. Navy and Oxblood.

OriginalityClosest: split-circle marks such as the PRI legislature panel (a green and red circle split down the middle) and Europ Assistance (navy and red halves, split horizontally). Ours offsets the halves vertically, which none of them do, in muted navy and oxblood.

DirectionSymbol: one disc cut in two, the navy half lifted, the oxblood half lowered.

Masthead or pitch deck?Masthead. Navy and oxblood are club and college colors; the offset keeps it from looking like a flag.

RationaleThe Tiger Data move without the tiger: a solid black disc sliced once, upright, to the right of center, with both pieces kept, a large body and a narrow sliver. Pure geometry; if it says anything it says that something small stands beside something large and belongs to it. Inter Display Bold, tight, black. On near-black it flips to paper.

Device, type, paletteDisc with one upright cut. Inter Display Bold. Black and White.

OriginalityClosest: Tiger Data (a black disc with a tiger head and diagonal stripes cut out) and Carraro. Ours has one straight upright cut and no picture; the searches found no disc with a single upright slice.

DirectionNegative space: a black disc sliced once, upright, right of center, both pieces kept.

Masthead or pitch deck?Masthead. A black disc with one cut is as sober as a bank's coin; nothing about it is playful.

RationaleA deep teal square with a quarter disc taken from its top-left corner, the radius exactly half the side, so the cut and the remaining mass are in simple proportion. It reads as a block that has been turned on a lathe, or a corner of an arch seen flat, but it is only a square and a circle. Bricolage Grotesque Bold, whose ink traps give the name a printed edge.

Device, type, paletteSquare minus a quarter disc. Bricolage Grotesque Bold. Deep Teal and White.

OriginalityClosest: the Ontario Association of Architects (a square with cut corners and a circle inside) and the many page-corner icons, which cut the top-right. Ours cuts top-left with a large radius, so it does not read as a document.

DirectionNegative space: a deep teal square with a quarter disc taken from its top-left corner.

Masthead or pitch deck?Borderline masthead. The teal and the cut feel architectural; in a brighter color it would tip into a tech logo, so it stays deep.

RationaleFrom the institution feeling: an ensign reduced to two bars. A broad navy bar over a shorter old-gold bar set flush right, the way a college or a regiment carries its colors, with no shield and no crest. The name in Fraunces SemiBold lowercase in navy, a bookish counterweight to the hard bars.

Device, type, paletteTwo bars, flush right. Fraunces SemiBold. Navy and Old Gold.

OriginalityClosest: university navy-and-gold identities (Marquette, Northern Arizona), which use shields, towers and mountains; no two-bar ensign was found. Flush-left bars would read as a text-align icon, so the gold bar is flush right and shorter.

DirectionOwn: a broad navy bar over a shorter gold bar, set flush right, like a college ensign.

Masthead or pitch deck?Masthead. Navy, old gold and a serif name are the colors of a college; the bars keep it modern.
