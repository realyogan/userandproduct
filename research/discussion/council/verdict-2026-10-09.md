# Council verdict, 9 Oct 2026

The question was whether the approach built over 8 and 9 October (the research method, the data-driven topic tree, the simplified site tree, the intent-first rule, the five sections, the launch waves and the cadence) is the right way to launch and grow userandproduct.com, and where it will fail.

Short answer: the research is sound and worth keeping. The launch plan built on it is not. The plan aims at the second goal (search traffic) while calling it the first (authority). It asks one author for more than one author can write. And it commits URLs and categories before a single article, reader or search impression exists. Keep the map, shrink the launch to about 12 pieces, put the owner's own experience at the center, and get a real site live within two weeks.

## Where the Council Agrees

**1. The plan optimizes for search while the stated goal is authority.** The Contrarian and the First Principles Thinker reached this on their own, the Outsider got there from the reader's side, and three of the five peer reviews named it the strongest point. The evidence is in the plan's own numbers:
- 67% of the 2.45M monthly searches that are really ours are "what is X" definitions.
- Every one of the 68 head terms has a Google AI Overview, and only 6 of 68 have a soft page one.
- Page one belongs to Reddit, LinkedIn, Atlassian, ProductPlan and Coursera.

A "What is product management?" article from a 15-year practitioner looks just like one from a content farm. It doesn't build the owner's name.

**2. The most important input is missing.** `owner_fit` is empty. Wave 1 is 81 articles and six months of writing, and it was chosen by score before the owner rated a single topic. The Contrarian, the First Principles Thinker and the Executor all flagged this. Even the Executor, who wants to move fastest, asks for the fit ratings first.

**3. One author can't do the arithmetic.** 81 articles at three a week takes 27 weeks with no slack, and the full tree takes about 14 months. On top of that, the plan opens five sections at launch: about 45 to 50 link entries, about 12 templates, a three-mode calculator and two book lists. The Contrarian and the Executor both say to plan for two substantial pieces a week and treat a third as a bonus. Every reviewer except the Expansionist's own lens criticized the Expansionist for adding work to a plan that was already too big.

**4. Search can't be the only channel.** The Contrarian, the First Principles Thinker, the Outsider and the Expansionist all point out that the plan has no LinkedIn, newsletter or community route. context.md already names LinkedIn as the distribution channel, but it never made it into the plan. The models in this niche are small (NN/g about 63K visits a month, Smashing about 17K), and neither built its authority through search volume.

**5. Template demand is the best overlap between the data and the owner.** Template searches make up 41% of user-story demand, 34% of roadmap demand and 33% of PRD demand. These topics are also among the easiest: Requirements and PRDs is KD 9, UX research is KD 8 and Product strategy is KD 5. The First Principles Thinker, the Expansionist and the Executor all land here. A strong practitioner opinion plus a usable template both ranks and builds credibility.

**6. Don't lock in 13 categories and 210 notional articles now.** Live URLs are costly to undo, and the scores shifted a lot within one day (prioritization went from 74.2 to 55.1, and the category count went from 11 to 13). The First Principles Thinker suggests launching with three or four categories. The Executor proposes flat `/article-slug/` permalinks so categories can move later without redirects.

## Where the Council Clashes

**Ship now or think first.** The Executor says the research is done, the next-session plan (re-score, v1.3 tree, retrofit document, content model) is weeks of preparation, and anything that doesn't have an article live within two weeks is still planning. The Contrarian and the First Principles Thinker say the launch set is aimed at the wrong target, so shipping it faster only speeds up the mistake. Both sides are right about different things. The Executor is right about tempo and the First Principles Thinker is right about direction. The fix is to settle direction in a single sitting, not over another research week, and then ship.

**Assets now or later.** The Expansionist sees templates, the sample-size calculator (14,990 adjusted searches), book lists and a Frameworks library as the moat, because an AI Overview can't hand anyone a working tool. The Expansionist wants about 8 of these assets at launch, an email gate on downloads, a yearly research report and a course. The Executor cuts Links, Books and Tools until 20 articles are live. The disagreement is real: the Expansionist is reasoning about what AI can't replace, and the Executor is reasoning about who builds it and when. The reviewers also pointed out that calculator and template page ones are already held by independent tool sites, Notion, Figma and ClickUp, so "assets escape AI" doesn't mean "assets are easy to rank."

**Field names or reader-task names for categories.** The Outsider says that "Product management", "Product strategy", "Product discovery" and "Project management" can't be told apart from outside the field, and proposes names like "Talking to users", "Deciding what to build" and "Writing specs and stories". The plan uses the field's own vocabulary because that is what the audience types and recognizes. Both are reasonable: the Outsider stands in for career switchers and founders, and the plan stands in for working practitioners.

**Glossary: weakness or format.** The Outsider uses "a glossary with templates attached" as a criticism. The Expansionist's peer review argues that short, linked definition pages are a cheap way to meet the 67% "what is X" demand and send those readers on to the opinion pieces. Both are partly right. A glossary is the wrong identity for the site and could be a useful supporting layer for it.

## Blind Spots the Council Caught

These came out only in peer review. None of the five advisors raised them in their own answers.

- **No stop rule and no success criteria.** Four of the five reviews flagged this. No one said what "working" looks like at week 8, month 3 or month 6, or what result would trigger a change of course. Six months of writing currently has no checkpoint.
- **An inventory of what the owner already has.** Three reviews flagged this: existing writing, talks, case studies, LinkedIn reach and network. That stock decides whether authority-first is fast or slow, and nobody asked about it.
- **Backlinks.** A new domain with no links won't rank even for "KD 8" terms. Difficulty scores assume a site that already has authority, and no response has a plan to earn links.
- **There is no live site.** Everything runs on local XAMPP. Nobody mentioned hosting, deployment, indexing or Search Console, so the Executor's "publish now" has nowhere to go yet.
- **Test the cadence before planning on it.** Write three real pieces, time them, and size the waves from the measured hours instead of the assumed three a week.
- **Being cited by AI answers.** Every advisor treated AI Overviews only as a threat. Pages with original frameworks, named methods and the owner's own data are the ones AI answers quote, and a citation builds authority even without a click.
- **Ask real readers.** Show the category names, a "start here" page and two sample articles to 5 to 10 working PMs and designers before committing.
- **Smaller points:** interviews with guest practitioners can stretch the one-author limit and widen the network; consulting, speaking and advisory leads are the fastest return on authority; an email gate on templates works against trust and leaves thin, unrankable pages.

## The Recommendation

Launch a small practitioner magazine built around the owner's own experience, using the research to choose titles and formats, not to choose topics. Go live within two weeks, publish about 12 pieces in the first six weeks, write two pieces a week, and treat LinkedIn as the main channel. Judge it at fixed checkpoints.

**Keep from the research:**
- The research store and the cache-first method.
- The intent labels. In particular, the finding that practitioner intent (template, compare, how-to) is small but winnable while definitional demand is large but mostly absorbed by AI Overviews.
- The SERP and difficulty data, used as a filter: it shows where Atlassian and Coursera already hold page one.
- The template-intent percentages.
- The five-year trends (45 of 68 head terms rising; PRD up 8.4x, roadmaps up 5.1x).
- The tree, kept as a backlog of 210 possible articles. It is not a publishing commitment.

**Change in the tree:**
- **Make owner_fit a gate, not a column.** A topic goes into the launch only if the owner rates it a strong fit. The score then ranks within those topics. Skip the v1.3 re-score.
- **Use flat `/article-slug/` permalinks.** This is the one irreversible decision, and flat URLs make every category choice reversible.
- **Don't show a category until it has at least 3 published articles.** Drop the three domain hubs for now. A short menu of live categories is enough.
- **Close the open calls:** Project management no (799,180 searches, but KD 39 and Atlassian on page one); Web design no; Analytics no. Keep Requirements inside Product discovery for now. Flat URLs mean it can split out later at no cost.
- **Keep the field's own category names**, because the audience is practitioners. Take the Outsider's point by opening each category page with one plain sentence about what the reader will find there.
- **Remove score and difficulty from anything a reader sees.** They are internal data.

**The launch set: about 12 pieces, not 81.**
- **4 signature pieces only the owner could write:** real decisions, failures, teardowns, and how the owner actually runs discovery or writes a PRD. These are the authority engine and the LinkedIn pieces.
- **6 practitioner pieces where the owner's experience overlaps the easiest demand,** each with its template on the same page:
  - PRD template and examples (about 4,500 searches a month across the phrase group; "prd template" 2,900 at KD 6; corrected 9 Oct after the second council caught the wrong 18,100 figure). Write this first.
  - User stories with a template.
  - Acceptance criteria (KD 10).
  - A roadmap piece (KD 10).
  - Two UX research pieces: surveys (KD 7) and usability testing (KD 8).
- **1 "What UX and product people search for" report,** built from data already paid for. This is the link-earning piece and the first answer to the backlink gap.
- **1 About and author page with Person schema,** plus a short "Start here" page.

**Sections open at launch:**
- **Articles.**
- **Templates, ungated,** only the 3 or 4 that come with launch articles.
- **No email gate.** Put a plain newsletter signup in the footer, sent monthly.
- **Books** opens around month 2, as one owner-written list. It is the cheapest asset that carries the owner's name.
- **Tools** opens around month 3 or 4, starting with the sample-size calculator.
- **Useful links** opens after 20 articles are live.

**Categories open at launch:** UX research, Product discovery (which holds PRDs, user stories and acceptance criteria) and Product strategy (roadmaps, prioritization, product-market fit). If the owner's signature list clusters somewhere else, for example Leadership and teams, that category replaces Product strategy, not adds to it.

**Cadence:** two substantial pieces a week, with three as a bonus. Aim for one signature piece and one search-led piece each week. Time the first three pieces and re-plan if they take more than 6 to 8 hours each. Skip the "what is X" head terms (What is product management, Product life cycle stages, Story points explained) until the site has authority, and never make them the identity.

**Channel:** LinkedIn for every piece, with a native post that carries the argument, not just a link. Add the monthly newsletter, the research report for links, and one practitioner interview a month from month 2.

**What to measure, by when:**

| By | Target | If missed |
|---|---|---|
| Day 14 | Site live on real hosting, Search Console verified, sitemap submitted, first piece published and indexed | Stop all other work until this is done |
| Week 3 | Measured hours per piece from the first three pieces | Re-size the cadence from the real number |
| Week 6 | 12 pieces live; LinkedIn engagement and new connections from practitioners tracked per piece; at least 3 inbound conversations (DMs, comments from peers, invitations) | Change formats, not the topics list |
| Month 3 | About 24 pieces; Search Console impressions rising month over month; at least 3 pieces in the top 30 for their target phrase; first 100 newsletter subscribers; at least 3 referring domains | If impressions are flat, shift weight further toward signature pieces and LinkedIn; don't add categories |
| Month 6 | About 48 pieces; the template pieces on page one or two; organic clicks growing month over month; the owner's name bringing at least one consulting, speaking or advisory inquiry | If neither search nor authority signals are moving, stop and re-run the council with real data |

Skip the reader test with 5 to 10 PMs and designers before launch. Run it at week 6, on the live site, so it doesn't become another planning gate.

## The One Thing to Do First

In one sitting, the owner writes down the 20 pieces only they could write: decisions they made, things that failed, how they actually work. Next to each, they list anything they already have for it (old talks, posts, documents, case material). Then they mark which ones overlap PRDs, user stories, roadmaps or UX research. That list is the missing `owner_fit`. It chooses the four signature pieces and confirms the six template pieces, and everything else in this verdict follows from it, starting with drafting the PRD article the same week.
