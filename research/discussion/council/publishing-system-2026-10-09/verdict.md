# Council verdict: the publishing system (proposal 2), 9 Oct 2026

The question: what are the gaps in the proposed newsroom of agents (research/explainers/publishing-system-proposal-2-2026-10-09.html), and how should the system be improved? Five advisors answered independently, five anonymous peer reviews followed, and the chairman synthesised. Inputs are in this folder: question.md, advisor-responses.md (anonymised), peer-reviews.md (with the key).

Short answer: the proposal spends ten agents on the cheap part (writing, drawing, checking) and gives the scarce part, the owner's judgment and stories, ten minutes per article. Rebuild it around the owner as the author in fact: agents interview, shape and check. Two pieces a week, not six. Prove the belt on one owner-written baseline and three test pieces before building any agent file.

## Where the Council Agrees

**The owner's experience is the scarce input, and the proposal treats it as garnish.** Four advisors said this on their own. The First Principles Thinker named the root cause: ten agents go to writing, drawing and checking, which are cheap; the owner's judgment gets about 10 minutes per article. The Contrarian called the ratio "ghostwriting at volume." The Outsider said a story stretched across 2,000 words "reads as garnish, not experience." The Expansionist said the experience is the product. The First Principles and Outsider reviews backed this.

**"Write six" is wrong. Two a week is right.** The Executor, the First Principles Thinker, the Contrarian and the Outsider all cut it to two, in line with the earlier council. The reasons differ. Six belts means about 40 agent runs, and some will stall. Six agent-written pieces a week on a new domain is the scaled-content pattern. Six drafts cannot get a real author's read in the Sunday hour; read from a summary, the owner becomes a rubber stamp.

**The build order is upside down.** The explorer comes first, but 210 ideas already exist. Ideas are not the bottleneck. Whether the belt can turn a story into a piece that reads like the owner is unproven. Prove that first.

**The gate grades itself.** The explorer writes its own "the top five pages do not teach" sentence, and a language model can always write that sentence. The proposal's own example ("one page, five sections") is a common angle, not an original one.

**The 90-day rank expectation is fantasy.** A domain with no links will fail it from day one, so the campaign manager's nudges would react to noise. Measure authority signals instead.

## Where the Council Clashes

**Grow the system or shrink it.** The Expansionist wants more: an interview agent, a yearly gap-map dataset, tags that flag product opportunities, a public series on running the newsroom. The Executor wants less: three making agents, not seven, and nothing built before three real articles. Three reviews named the Expansionist's additions as its blind spot; the Expansionist review named the Executor's pure subtraction. The sequence settles it: the Executor is right about now, the Expansionist is right about month six.

**Does the earlier council's "two a week" settle anything?** The Contrarian review says that figure assumed the owner writes, so it says nothing about what agents can carry. Fair as logic. In practice the limit is how fast the owner can supply real insight and read drafts as their author, and that limit holds whoever types.

**Disclosure.** The Expansionist wants the newsroom run openly. The Outsider says hiding how pieces are made is the bigger trust risk. The no-attribution rule is the owner's and the council does not overturn it. The clean way out is to make the owner the author in fact: he supplies the argument, the stories and the judgment; agents shape and check; then there is nothing to disclose and nothing to expose. Park the public newsroom series.

## Blind Spots the Council Caught

- **Invented anecdotes cannot be caught.** The checker verifies claims against sources. The owner's memory is not a source, so a padded story passes. (Contrarian, stressed by two reviews.)
- **Confidentiality.** Fifteen years of stories involve former employers, clients and colleagues. Nobody proposed a "who could recognise this?" step. (Two reviews.)
- **The stories run out.** At two a week, the best stories are gone in a year or two.
- **No email list.** LinkedIn is rented reach. Books and courses need an audience the owner owns.
- **No real readers.** Every check is done by an agent, the owner or a peer. No reader feedback loop and no public corrections policy.
- **Operations.** An open session routing about 40 hand-offs will overflow its context. Nothing schedules the weekly jobs. Per-article cost is unpriced. There is no baseline piece written by the owner to judge "reads like me" against.
- **Trust and distribution limit a new domain, not production.** The first 12 pieces may need very little machine at all.

## The Recommendation

Rebuild the proposal around the owner as author. Agents act as interviewer, shaper and checker.

**Keep:** one folder per article; the managing editor routing through files, never agent to agent; the per-desk feedback files; "nothing publishes without the owner"; the learning-sentence gate; volume ranks ideas and never admits them; the writing-style and thumbnail rulebooks; the honest-limits section; LinkedIn packs posted by hand.

**Change, in this order:**

1. **Baseline and test (weeks 1 to 3).** The owner writes one piece himself as the baseline. Then three articles go through plain prompts with no agent files: researcher, writer, reviewer. Time every owner minute. Show all four pieces blind to one peer and ask, "Does this sound like someone who's done it?" Build nothing else until this is done.
2. **Flip the input.** Every article starts with a 30-minute interview per piece, run by an interview prompt inside the making desk, not a new desk. The owner dictates the core argument, the story and the numbers. The transcript becomes stories.md: tagged, reusable, each with a status of publishable, disguise or private.
3. **New hard rules.** (a) No first-person detail, number or event the owner did not supply; the reviewer marks every first-person sentence with its source line in the transcript. (b) A confidentiality check on every story: "Who could recognise this?" The owner signs off. (c) The editor's question becomes "Could only this author have written this?" A "no" goes back to the owner, not to the writer.
4. **Making desk: four agents, not seven.** Researcher, writer, reviewer (checker, SEO review and edit merged into one pass with three sections in checks.md), and illustrator (figures plus thumbnail). Split an agent off only when it fails twice in the same place.
5. **Cadence.** Two a week. A third only when the owner has a story ready for it. Water marks: keep 6 approved ideas, refill below 4. One article per session, so context never overflows.
6. **Planning.** Seed the backlog CSV from the existing 210-topic tree. The story bank becomes the first source of ideas; competitor gaps and volume filter them. A second agent, not the explorer, checks each learning sentence against the top five pages. Weekly sitemap diffs wait until launch.
7. **Add a newsletter** from the first published piece. Add a corrections note on the site.
8. **Performance.** Delete "top 10 in 90 days." For the first six months measure indexation, LinkedIn replies from people who matter, inbound messages, subscribers and citations. Build the campaign manager only after 90 days of Search Console data.
9. **Park:** reels, the public newsroom series, the gap-map dataset and product tagging. Revisit the Expansionist's ideas at month six. They are good, and they depend on the core working.

Rulebooks to write, in order: the interview and story-bank rules (with confidentiality), the merged review, illustrations, WordPress publishing, LinkedIn. Performance comes last.

## The One Thing to Do First

Pick one idea from the existing tree, tell its story aloud for 30 minutes, run it through three plain prompts (researcher, writer, reviewer), and time every minute you spend, before building a single agent file.
