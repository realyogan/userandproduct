# Five anonymized advisor responses

**Response A:**

The proposal treats the owner's experience as an input to the article belt. I think it's the product, and the system undersells it in four places.

1. The story bank is the most valuable file in the building, and it's only a queue-jumper. In section 3 it is "a file of the stories you have told," fed by about 10 minutes of telling per article. Turn it around. Add a weekly 20-minute interview agent that asks the owner pointed questions ("the worst roadmap review you sat through," "a metric you killed") and files tagged, reusable stories. Fifteen years of material, mined properly, becomes the site's moat. It also becomes the book manuscript the BRD puts under "later," the course syllabus and the talk deck. Every product on the roadmap draws from that one store.

2. The newsroom is itself the flagship content. A product leader running a twelve-agent team with expectations, water marks, stop signs and a scorecard is exactly where UX, product management and business meet. "How I run a magazine as a product" is a series only this author can write. Running it openly earns authority and links far faster than any explainer on PRD length.

3. The explorer throws away original research. Reading the top five pages for 210 topics, and logging what each one misses (no example, no numbers, no template), adds up to a dataset: a gap map of the UX and PM web. Publish it once a year. AI answer engines and journalists quote original data, and the earlier council already said so.

4. Section 2's "Format" field should flag product opportunities. When the competitor notes keep saying "no template" or "no numbers," that's a template or calculator waiting to be built, and those are the BRD's later revenue lines. Tag them from article one, so the tools directory and template shop build up as a byproduct.

Bonus: flip distribution. Today LinkedIn only receives finished articles. Use it as the test market instead. Post the learning sentence first, and the posts that land earn a full article. The "Expectation" field should also score authority signals (citations, inbound DMs, invitations), not just rank and clicks. Authority is goal number one, and right now nothing in the system measures it.

**Response B:**

The build order is upside down. Step 1 is the explorer with the weekly sitemap compare, but the backlog is not your bottleneck. You already have about 210 scored ideas in the topic tree. That covers ten-plus weeks of backlog even at six a week. The thing nobody has proven is section 5: can the belt turn your 10-minute story into a piece that reads like you? Build that first. Concretely, on Monday morning: take one idea from the existing tree, write its learning sentence by hand, tell the story, then run researcher, writer and editor by hand as three prompts with no agent files. Time every step and count your own minutes. That one run tells you more than seven sessions of infrastructure.

Seven making-desk agents is about four too many for version one. Every hand-off through the managing editor costs a context reload. The checker, SEO reviewer and editor all read the same draft and the same rulebooks. Merge them into one review pass with three sections in checks.md. Fold the thumbnail maker into the illustrator's run. Researcher, writer, reviewer: three agents. Split one off only when it fails twice in the same spot.

"Write six" in one Sunday sitting is a demo number, not a routine. Six belts times seven stations is about 40 subagent runs. Each one reads the top five pages, so you are carrying a lot of context. Expect two or three to stall halfway. The resume-from-last-file design helps, but someone still has to notice the stall. And six one-page summaries plus six drafts is not the "short review." It is two to three hours of careful reading, or rubber-stamping. The earlier council said two a week. Start at two, which is question 1's "three" minus one.

Things to cut or park until they have data to act on: the expectation line (you cannot write "top 10 in 90 days" honestly for a domain with zero links), the six-month review date, trend, and questions 5 to 7. The campaign manager and reels are already parked. The publisher is blocked on WordPress anyway.

The first useful build is the backlog CSV seeded from the existing tree, the stories.md file, and the per-desk feedback files. Then run three real articles end to end. Then decide.

**Response C:**

You're asking how to improve the machine. The question underneath is what input is actually scarce. The proposal is built around the wrong answer.

The owner's first goal is authority: the owner's 15 years backing the site. So the scarce input is the owner's judgment: decisions made, failures, numbers, frameworks nobody else has. Writing, drawing, checking and publishing are cheap now. The proposal spends ten agents on the cheap part and gives the scarce part a few lines, about 10 minutes per article (section 4, step 3). Your story bank is the sixth of six sources, a tiebreaker that "jumps the queue." That's upside down.

Look at where originality comes from in this design. The explorer reads the top five pages and writes "the reader learns X, which the top five pages do not teach." That makes every idea a reaction to competitors. It finds the hole in their coverage, not the thing only you know. Your stated test is "I haven't seen this approach." The PRD example passes the gate by being shorter than a 12-section template. That's an angle anyone could have, not your authority.

Rebuild from the ground up:

1. Start with your knowledge, not the backlog. Add an interviewer desk: a 30-minute weekly session where an agent questions you. "Tell me about a launch that failed. What did the numbers say? What would you do now?" It turns your answers into a knowledge bank of claims, frameworks and data. Ideas come out of that bank first. Competitor gaps and keyword volume then pick the best of those ideas; they don't generate them.
2. Change the editor's question. "Does it deliver the promised learning?" should become "Could only this author have written this?" If the answer is no, the draft goes back to you, not to the writer.
3. Measure the goal you actually hold. The campaign manager scores rank by day 90. For a new domain with no links, that measures the wrong thing. Track authority instead: LinkedIn replies from people who matter, inbound messages, citations, quotes in AI answers.
4. Six a week is the throughput goal talking. The earlier council said two a week, tested on three real pieces first. The real limit is how fast you can supply real insight, not how fast agents can write. Publish at that rate.
5. Build later. Write three pieces with light help first. Only then build the agents those three pieces showed you need. Ten agents built before the first article exists are guesses.

**Response D:**

The fatal flaw is the ratio. The proposal asks the owner for "a few lines, or a voice note transcript" per article, about "10 minutes", and has the machine write the other 2,000 words under his name with no attribution. The owner's first goal is authority in both directions. Ten minutes of input inside six machine drafts a week is ghostwriting at volume. The earlier council said first-hand specifics have to carry each piece. Here they are a garnish. The worst failure is a writer agent padding a three-line story with details that never happened. The checker can't catch that, because it "verifies every claim... against the sources", and the owner's memory is not a source. One invented anecdote under a 15-year practitioner's byline does more damage than a hundred ranking failures.

"Write six" ignores the council from the same morning: two a week is realistic, and test the cadence on three pieces first. Six agent-written articles a week on a new domain with no links is the pattern Google's scaled-content policy targets. It also breaks the Sunday promise. The owner has to read six full drafts, then fix and re-read them. That is not "1 to 2 hours". Either he reads every line or he starts approving from the one-page summaries. Once he approves from summaries, he is a rubber stamp.

The gate grades itself. "If the explorer cannot write that sentence honestly": a language model can always write that sentence. Even the sample fails it: "cut a PRD to one page in five sections" is advice you can find all over the web. Real originality comes from the owner's frameworks and data. Reading the top five pages can't produce it.

The expectations are fantasy. "Top 10... within 90 days" on a domain with no links is exactly what the earlier council said won't happen. So the campaign manager will score every article as failing from day one, and its "pause a category" nudges will just react to noise.

The build order is upside down. It builds the explorer first, but there are already 210 ideas, so ideas are not the bottleneck. The owner's stories are. The fix: build the story bank first and run an interview agent that pulls depth out of him. Cap output at two pieces a week. Have the owner write or dictate the core argument himself. Ban any first-person detail he did not supply. Build the campaign manager once there is real traffic to measure.

**Response E:**

Picture me as a reader. I land on "How to write a PRD engineers read to the end." The site sells itself on one man's 15 years. What would make me trust the piece? A real PRD of his, something that went wrong, and his judgment about why. Now look at how this machine makes it. The writer is a program working from "a few lines" or "a voice note transcript" that took you 10 minutes (Section 11, Q1). Everything else (structure, examples, the framework, the "five sections") comes from a researcher that read five competitor pages. I'd get a competent article with a story pasted on top. Readers can tell. A ten-minute anecdote stretched across 2,000 words reads as garnish, not experience.

Here's the curse of knowledge. To you, "the owner approves every article" means the owner wrote it. To me it doesn't. Approving a draft is not the same as authoring it. Your goal is "the site backs the owner," but if a hiring manager or client ever learns that twelve agents write it, the site works against you. Your no-AI-attribution rule makes that worse, not better: hiding how the pieces are made is the thing that would wreck trust if it came out.

The learning sentence gate is clever, but look who grades it: the explorer, the same program that wants the board full (Section 2). "Which the top five pages do not teach" will get written honestly about as often as a salesperson calls their own lead weak. The example even proves it. "One page, five sections" isn't new. It's a listicle angle.

Notice too what's missing in the time budget. Reading six drafts properly, as their named author, takes far more than the "read the summary" step in Section 4. I can't see any time in the plan for you to rewrite.

What I'd change:
- Flip the roles. You talk for 30 to 45 minutes per piece and the machine shapes that. Don't let the machine draft and then add you.
- Cut "write six" to two. That's what the earlier council said and what you can actually stand behind.
- Have a second agent, not the explorer, check every learning sentence against the top five pages.
- Add one test before anything else: show three drafts to a peer in your field without saying how they were made. Ask: "Does this sound like someone who's done it?"
