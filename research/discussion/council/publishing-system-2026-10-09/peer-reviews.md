# Peer reviews

## Review by the Contrarian lens

1. Strongest: D. It is the only response that names a failure the proposal's own checks cannot catch. A writer agent pads a three-line story with details that never happened, and the checker passes it, because it checks claims against sources and the owner's memory is not a source. The fix is cheap and testable: "ban any first-person detail he did not supply." B comes close because it proposes an experiment (one timed manual run) rather than an opinion. Most of the others restate the earlier council's "two a week" as if it were evidence. That figure assumed the owner writes the pieces, so it does not settle what agents can carry.

2. Biggest blind spot: A. It answers "too much machinery" with more machinery: an interview agent, a yearly gap dataset, product tagging, and a public series on the newsroom. Its point 2, "run it openly," clashes with the no-AI-attribution rule, and A never notices. E sees the same tension and says hiding how the pieces are made is the bigger risk. The council should settle that disclosure question outright, not leave it implied. C, D and E also each prescribe an interview agent, which is another agent built before anything is proven.

3. What all five missed: the stories belong partly to other people. Fifteen years of "worst roadmap review" and "the metric I killed" involve former employers, clients and colleagues. Mining them weekly and publishing at volume brings risks around confidentiality agreements, people being recognisable, and reputation. None of the five proposes a sanitising step or an owner check that asks "who could recognise this?" Second miss: the supply of stories runs out. Interviewing at two pieces a week uses up the stories that matter most within a year or two, and nobody plans for what happens then.

## Review by the First Principles lens

1. Strongest: C. It gets to the root problem. The scarce input is the owner's judgment, and the proposal spends ten agents on the cheap part. C also finds the deeper flaw in the gate. Ideas come from competitor gaps, so every piece is a reaction to someone else rather than something only this author knows. Its fix puts things in the right order: the owner's knowledge bank generates ideas, and search filters them. "Could only this author have written this?" is the right test for the editor. D comes a close second for one point nobody else made: invented anecdotes can't be caught, because the checker never treats the owner's memory as a source.

2. Biggest blind spot: A. It agrees the experience is the product, then adds more on top: an interview agent, a yearly gap dataset, product tags, and a public "newsroom" series. It never asks about volume or whether the machine is the real author. The "how I run a magazine with agents" series also undercuts authority, as E's point shows: once readers learn twelve agents write the site, the owner's byline counts for less.

3. What all five missed: Confidentiality. Fifteen years of stories, numbers and real PRDs mostly belong to former employers and clients. Any story bank or interviewer needs a rule for what can be published, anonymised or disguised. Without one, the scarce input shrinks. The real bottleneck: a new domain with no links is limited by trust and distribution, not production. The first 12 pieces may need no system at all. The owner's hours may be better spent on LinkedIn, guest posts and relationships.

## Review by the Expansionist lens

1. Strongest: A. It is the only response that treats the system as an asset that compounds, not just an article belt to slim down or fix. Four things build on each other: the interview-fed story bank also becomes the book, the course and the talks. Running the newsroom becomes a series only this author can write. The explorer's notes on what the top five pages miss become a yearly gap-map dataset for journalists and AI answer engines to quote. Gaps marked "no template" or "no numbers" seed the BRD's later revenue lines. A also turns LinkedIn into a test market and asks for authority signals to be measured.

2. Biggest blind spot: B. Its operational fixes are sound: build in a different order, merge reviewers, start at two a week. But it is pure subtraction. It never asks what the owner's 15 years could become beyond an article, so the story bank stays a plain file. Its plan protects the owner's time and leaves most of the upside unclaimed. D has the same tendency, to a lesser degree.

3. What all five missed: No email list. No response puts a newsletter in the system. LinkedIn is rented reach, and the BRD's books, courses and templates need an audience the owner owns. The owner's network: interviewing other practitioners would bring in links, people sharing the pieces, and authority by association. A new domain with no links needs that. The owner's own documents: real PRDs, decks and post-mortems, with names removed, are first-hand proof that no agent can make up.

## Review by the Outsider lens

1. Strongest: E. A stranger can follow it without knowing any jargon. It starts from a reader landing on one article and asks what would make that reader trust it. It also names the risk the others skirt: the owner approving a draft is not the owner writing it. If anyone learns that twelve agents write the site, the no-attribution rule turns into a trust problem. Its test is cheap and anyone would find it convincing: show three drafts to a peer in the field without saying how they were made. D comes a close second. Its point about a made-up anecdote under a 15-year byline is the most vivid risk in the set.

2. Biggest blind spot: B. It is sharp on operations (merge agents, start at two a week, prove the writing step first). But it treats the system as a throughput problem. It never asks whether machine-drafted pieces under the owner's name build authority at all, and the owner ranked authority as goal one. A has a smaller blind spot. It says to run the newsroom "openly" as flagship content and doesn't notice that this clashes with the no-AI-attribution rule.

3. What all five missed: real readers. Every check is done by agents, the owner or a peer, and nobody plans to put drafts in front of actual target readers. There is also no channel for readers to answer back, such as replies, a newsletter or comments. So the owner's own test ("I haven't seen this approach, this is useful") is never measured from the reader's side. Also missing: a public corrections policy for when a published piece turns out to be wrong.

## Review by the Executor lens

1. Strongest: B. It is the only response you could act on Monday morning. Take one idea from the existing tree, write the learning sentence by hand and tell the story. Then run researcher, writer and editor as three plain prompts, with no agent files, and time the owner's minutes. It cuts the making desk from seven agents to three, with a clear rule for adding more: split one off only after it fails twice in the same spot. It names the first build (a backlog CSV seeded from the tree, stories.md, the feedback files), then three real articles, then a decision. D and E reach the same verdict. E's blind peer test is the best single check in the set.

2. Biggest blind spot: A. It adds work: an interview agent, a public "newsroom as product" series, a yearly gap-map dataset, tagging for product opportunities, LinkedIn as a test market. It never asks whether the core belt can produce one good article, and it leaves "write six" in place. These are good ideas for month six, but none of them is something to do on Monday.

3. What all five missed: how the system actually runs each week. "The main session routes every hand-off" means an interactive session has to be open for every relay. Six articles at about seven stations each is roughly 40 hand-offs. That will overflow one session's context. Nothing schedules the weekly sitemap diffs or Search Console reads. No response priced the agent runs per article against the "free or cheap" rule. And no one asked for a baseline to judge "reads like the owner" against, such as one piece the owner wrote himself.
