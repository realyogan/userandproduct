# Peer review, First Principles lens

**1. Strongest: E.** E is the only response that goes back to the stated goal order (authority first, traffic second) and shows that the whole method optimised for the second goal. It then keeps the research rather than throwing it out: the data titles and formats the pieces but doesn't choose them. It also gives a smaller rebuild the owner can act on: 20 to 30 pieces only the owner could write, 3 or 4 categories, and LinkedIn as the channel. C reaches the same diagnosis with sharper evidence (the score swings, the Ads check that isn't independent), but it ends in questions, not a direction. A is the best on execution, but it never asks whether the launch set is right.

**2. Biggest blind spot: D.** D treats the binding constraint, one author's hours, as if it didn't exist. A and C both show that 81 articles is already more than one person can keep up. D adds about 8 assets on top, plus a Frameworks hub, a yearly research report, an email gate and a course. It also reads raw demand as opportunity: the calculator and template page ones are held by independent tool sites, Notion and Figma, which is the same problem with being displaced that D says assets escape. An email gate on templates also works against authority-building, because it adds friction to the very things meant to win trust.

**3. What all five missed:**
- **An inventory of what the owner already has.** That means existing writing, talks, case studies, LinkedIn reach and network. The cheapest authority is the authority that already exists. The plan should start from that stock, not from a blank tree.
- **Success and kill criteria.** None of the five names what "working" looks like at month 3 or 6, or which signal would trigger a change of direction.
- **Links.** A new domain with no backlinks won't rank even for "KD 8" terms, and no response has a plan for earning links.

Together these make the right next step a short, measured test, not a choice between launch plans.
