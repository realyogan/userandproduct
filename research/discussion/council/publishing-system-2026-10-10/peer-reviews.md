# Peer reviews (round 2, anonymous)

Each reviewer saw the five responses as letters A to E, in a shuffled order, and did not know who wrote which. The key, revealed after the round:

- Response A: The Outsider
- Response B: The Contrarian
- Response C: The Executor
- Response D: The First Principles Thinker
- Response E: The Expansionist

Reviewers 1 to 5 read with these lenses: general; actionable in two weeks; the authority goal; claims checked against the proposal; respect for the owner's own decisions.

## Review 1

**1. Strongest: B.** It names the central flaw most precisely. Nothing on the belt supplies the X the gate promises, and the hard rule bans only first-person claims, so invented third-person teams, numbers and quotes slip past a checker that has no source to fail them against. Every fix can be tested: an origin field for X, the hard rule extended to all invented specifics, a cold check against the top five, a timed first window, making-desk-first. Its "Top 10 within 90 days" complaint holds: the proposal's example card says exactly that. D finds the same root, and its voice-answered seed questions are the best single fix here; B covers more.

**2. Biggest blind spot: E.** Every idea adds output: a principles canon, an asset on every card, a published field report, more takes, a newsletter pipeline, shelf entries. It never asks whether drafts hold real insight, or counts the owner's hours, the proposal's stated gate. Thirty templates means thirty more reviews. C is the mirror image: sharp on logistics, silent on quality.

**3. What all five missed.**
- **Search-policy risk.** Fifty agent-written pieces, three or four a day, with no AI disclosure, is the pattern Google's scaled-content-abuse policy targets. A manual action on a new domain would wipe out goal two.
- **No originality check.** The writer works from the top five pages and from books, yet no station tests drafts for close paraphrase of those sources. That is a copyright risk and an authority risk.

## Review 2

**1. Strongest: C.** It is the only response you can run on Monday. It finds real breaks (checked against the proposal): a plain HTML page cannot save corrections, coverage mode has nothing to measure before a domain exists, and the theme, plugin and section pages ("no empty corners") are missing from the build order. It gives a dated path: three hand-made cards, a merged belt, timed reads, WordPress alongside, domain bought now. It is weak on quality, so add B's "where X comes from" field and D's seed questions to that first window.

**2. Biggest blind spot: E.** It adds a principles canon, an asset per card, a published field report, more takes, a newsletter pipeline and course outlines. Each is good for year one; each grows step 1, which C shows is already five or six sessions with zero articles. E never asks whether the belt can make one good draft yet, and it pushes takes without addressing the risk of misquoting rivals.

**3. What all five missed.**
- **Search policy.** Fifty agent-written pieces at three or four a day on a new domain, then a daily drip, is the pattern Google's scaled-content-abuse policy targets. Printables is no precedent for teaching articles. Set a floor for the owner's real input per piece and a gentler soft-launch pace.
- **The owner misses a window.** Nothing unapproved goes live, so one sick week empties the calendar. Keep a buffer of approved pieces and a rule for slipped dates.

## Review 3

**1. Strongest: D.** It asks the right question for goal one: where does the owner's judgment enter the page? It shows every input is public except a note about form, and uses the proposal's own "real product team" example as proof. Its fix is cheap: two or three practice questions per card, answered by voice in minutes, with cards labeled "seeded" or "synthesis". It also sees that a ramp gated on less owner time trades authority for volume. B gives the same diagnosis plus the best guard (no invented third-person examples), but D says how X gets supplied, not only how fakes get blocked.

**2. Biggest blind spot: C.** Its build points are sound (the app needs a save endpoint, the theme is unscheduled, the explorer can wait), but all of them serve throughput. Its fastest path makes the same synthesis sooner and never asks whether the first three drafts carry the author. It even cuts takes, the ideas built on lived experience.

**3. What all five missed: positions, not just facts.** The hard rule covers first-person claims; B adds invented examples. But every "do this, never that" under his byline reads as his judgment, with or without an "I". An agent's recommendation he does not hold is a borrowed opinion with his name on it; his network will notice. The editor should list each position a draft takes; the owner approves, rewords or strikes each one before reading the prose. That list is also his fastest review.

## Review 4

**1. Strongest: B.** Its claims check out against the extract. The card does promise "Top 10 within 90 days, 50 clicks by day 120" (line 171). The draft does "come back to you" (line 587), so the 30 to 40 minutes leaves out the re-read. Every fix is cheap and testable: a "where X comes from" field, a cold check of X against the top five, a timed first window. D makes the same point with a better fix (seed questions) but less proof. C is right that the build order has no theme, plugin, hosting or domain. Its "coverage has nothing to measure" goes too far: planned against published and orphan pages work locally.

**2. Biggest blind spot: E.** Every suggestion adds output: an asset per article, quarterly reports, more takes, a newsletter. None is weighed against the owner's review hours, which the proposal names as the gate. "More takes" ignores the takes rule: the owner must have lived the opposite, and that can't be scheduled. E never asks whether X exists.

**3. Missed by all five:**
- Google's scaled-content-abuse policy. Fifty agent-written pieces in two weeks on a new domain with no links is the pattern it targets. Printables, a site of printable template pages, is no precedent for bylined expert articles.
- Consistency across articles. Nothing checks that piece 31 doesn't contradict piece 4. Cards link only to pieces already written, so earlier pieces never get links back to the later pieces in the same stock.

## Review 5

**1. Strongest: D.** It keeps the owner's choices and makes them work. The role stays direction, not stories: D adds two or three practice questions per card, answered in minutes and stated as rules, with no first person needed. That gives the learning sentence a source without bringing back the story burden. It also aims effort at the launch: seed the ten pieces the LinkedIn network will open first. The "seeded versus synthesis" label and the editor's second question cost little. B finds the same hole, but its fix bars "derived" cards from approval, which works more like a veto.

**2. Biggest blind spot: C.** Its catches are real and checkable: the page cannot save without a PHP endpoint, the theme work is not scheduled, and coverage mode has nothing to measure before launch. But it treats the problem as throughput only. Its fast path proves the belt runs and times the reads. It never asks whether drafts carry the owner's judgment, which is goal one. It also cuts the explorer, takes and the books door, which the owner chose on purpose.

**3. What all five missed:**

- **Consistency across fifty pieces.** Drafts made in parallel will give conflicting advice (one PRD piece says one page, another says three). Nothing checks a piece against the rest of the site.
- **Google's scaled-content policy.** Fifty machine-written pieces on a new domain within two weeks, with no disclosure, is the pattern that policy targets. Printables is a different case.
